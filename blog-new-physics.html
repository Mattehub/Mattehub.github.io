New Data, New Systems: New Physics

Thoughts · 2026

The early 1900s were a turning point. Experiments started to probe systems that classical physics could not explain, and the result was the quantum revolution. We are at a similar moment today, not because the equations are the same, but because once again we have new systems and new data that challenge our understanding.

For most of the last century we measured isolated variables: the current in a wire, the brightness of a bulb, the position of a planet. Now we can measure social, neural, and technological systems at scale, with thousands of interacting parts recorded in parallel. These datasets are qualitatively different, and they demand a new science that can describe collective behavior, emergence, and multi-level organization.

The physics of complex systems: networked, data-rich, and grounded in how interactions shape collective outcomes.

This is exciting because it is not just about refining old theories. It is about building a new conceptual toolbox, one that can explain phenomena we could not even measure a few decades ago. The data are here. The systems are here. The science is still being invented.

AI is part of this story. Modern AI systems are complex, high-dimensional, and deeply interactive. Understanding them is a challenge for physics and mathematics, not only because the models are large, but because their behavior emerges from the interaction of many components at once.

It is easy to treat AI as a black box, especially given the speed at which models evolve. But mechanistic interpretability is beginning to reveal internal structure: induction heads, signs of superposition, and tools for tracing causal pathways through a network are now well-studied building blocks.
